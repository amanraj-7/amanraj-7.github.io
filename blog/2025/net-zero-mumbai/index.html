Is net-zero the right goal for Mumbai?

Created on November 17, 2025

2025   ·   climate   cities   policy   ·   research

“Net-zero cities” have become one of the most influential ideas in urban climate policy. In an influential review, Seto et al. (2021) lay out what it would take for cities to move from low-carbon to net-zero carbon. It is a valuable framework, but is it the right one for every city?

I looked at that question for Mumbai, one of the world’s largest and densest cities. My conclusion is that the net-zero concept is not feasible for Mumbai at present. That is not because climate mitigation does not matter. It is because Mumbai’s socioeconomic conditions, infrastructure, governance, and development priorities do not fit the assumptions the framework is built on.

The Mumbai skyline and the Bandra-Worli Sea Link. Photo: Yoyosrk, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

What the net-zero framework asks of cities

Seto et al. argue that reaching net-zero requires cities to transform their energy supply, building materials, urban transportation, and land-use planning, while also decarbonizing the upstream supply chains they depend on, including electricity, food, materials, and water. They emphasize integrated spatial planning, compact urban form, behavioral change, and decades of consistent governance to break long-standing carbon lock-in.

For Mumbai, those assumptions are fundamentally misaligned with the city’s built environment and development path.

Mumbai’s most urgent problems are elsewhere

Mumbai’s most pressing challenges are deeply rooted ones:

  • Overcrowding, driven by migration from across India
  • Inadequate housing, with more than 40% of residents living in informal settlements (Páv, 2023)
  • Chronic flooding during the monsoon (Bearpark et al., 2025)
  • Overstressed transportation networks (The Hindu, 2025)
  • Persistent gaps in basic services such as water, drainage, and waste management
Monsoon waterlogging on Linking Road in Bandra, Mumbai. Photo: PlaneMad, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Net-zero ambitions demand large capital investments in advanced, low-carbon technologies. Against these needs, they become secondary to resilience, public health, and social equity. For a city still struggling to provide reliable, safe, and equitable basic infrastructure, diverting limited resources toward an idealized net-zero pathway risks deepening existing vulnerabilities rather than reducing them.

Elphinstone Road station on Mumbai's suburban railway, one of the most heavily used commuter networks in the world. Photo: A.Savin, Wikimedia Commons, Free Art License.

Mumbai does not control its supply chains

The framework expects cities to decarbonize not only their own emissions but also the upstream emissions from electricity, fuel, food, water, waste, and construction materials. Mumbai has little authority over any of these systems.

  • Electricity: the city’s power mix is largely set by the state and national governments, and India’s electricity still relies heavily on coal, at around 70% (IEA).
  • Goods and materials: most of Mumbai’s food, construction materials, and manufactured goods come from far beyond city limits.
  • Trade: its port and industrial activity are tied into global shipping networks.
A coal-fired power station in West Bengal. Coal still generates most of India's electricity, and a city like Mumbai has little say over the mix. Photo: Duke Das, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

The city government lacks the institutional, legal, and economic power to enforce decarbonization across such extensive and complex supply chains. Without that power, it cannot realistically meet the net-zero goals Seto et al. describe.

Mumbai is not Canberra, Chongming, or Denver

The case studies in Seto et al., Canberra, Chongming, and Denver, are cities that are wealthy, stable, and in some cases newly planned. Their main climate challenge is cutting their own, often high, per-capita emissions.

Mumbai’s challenges are different. As a dense, low-lying coastal city, it faces rising seas that threaten its drainage system, more intense monsoon rains that cause severe flooding, and extreme heat waves that endanger public health. Its planning and financial resources should therefore go first to adaptation and resilience, such as upgrading colonial-era stormwater drains and securing its water supply. Those priorities look very different from the pathways in the article, such as electrifying heating and mobility or carbon valorization.

Little room for carbon sequestration

Carbon sequestration is a key part of most net-zero strategies, but the opportunity in Mumbai is small. Seto et al. show that even cities with extensive green areas offset less than 1% of their emissions through vegetation, and Mumbai has some of the lowest green space per person of any major city (Observer Research Foundation, 2021). With intense competition for land, high land prices, and ongoing expansion, the city cannot meaningfully expand green infrastructure for carbon uptake. Any sequestration would be negligible compared with its emissions.

Governance and finance barriers

Seto et al. identify carbon lock-in from long-lived infrastructure as a central challenge, and argue that breaking it requires systemic transformation backed by polycentric, multilevel cooperation and massive finance. In Mumbai, those barriers are close to absolute.

  • Fragmented governance: responsibility is split among the Maharashtra state government, the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation, and the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority, which makes coordinated, systemic change politically very difficult.
  • Finance: the article cites an OECD estimate of \$6.9 trillion per year needed globally for climate infrastructure. Mumbai’s budget struggles to cover even basic services.

For Mumbai, carbon lock-in is not just a challenge to overcome. It defines the city’s reality.

An adaptation-first approach

Given these structural, spatial, and institutional limits, net-zero is not the most useful planning framework for Mumbai today. An adaptation-first approach would serve the city better, prioritizing flood resilience, drainage upgrades, heat mitigation, and affordable housing.

That does not mean ignoring emissions. Practical low-carbon steps still make sense, even if they fall short of net-zero:

  • Improving energy efficiency
  • Electrifying bus fleets
  • Better waste management
  • Expanding public transportation

Mumbai’s climate policy should put social equity, resilience, and basic services for everyone first, rather than setting emissions targets the city does not yet have the capacity to meet.
