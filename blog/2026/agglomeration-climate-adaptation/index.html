Do big cities make climate adaptation easier or harder?

Created on November 05, 2026

2026   ·   climate   cities   adaptation   ·   research

Cities concentrate people, capital, and ideas in one place. Economists call the benefits of that concentration agglomeration effects, and they are a big part of why cities drive economic growth. But concentration cuts both ways when it comes to climate change.

In this post, I look at how agglomeration can make cities more resilient, and how it can also lock them into high-risk situations.

How agglomeration helps cities adapt

The forces that make cities economically productive can also power climate adaptation. Proximity encourages innovation, makes resilient infrastructure cheaper per person, and supports the specialized expertise that complex environmental problems demand.

Innovation and knowledge

The biggest advantage of urban density is how quickly ideas move. Cities are hubs of innovation because frequent interactions let people combine existing ideas into new ones, and that kind of collaboration is exactly what climate adaptation needs.

  • Knowledge spillovers. When skilled workers, universities, and industries sit close together, ideas spread easily. An urban planner, a data scientist, and a materials engineer can work together on data-driven, heat-resilient infrastructure in a way that would be much harder if they were scattered.
  • Concentrations of talent. Successful cities such as New York and London attract and develop skilled workers. That pool of engineers, climate scientists, urban planners, and emergency managers provides the expertise to design and deliver complex adaptation projects, and world-class universities act as anchors for it.

Economies of scale in infrastructure

Density makes large public investments cheaper per person, which matters a great deal for the expensive infrastructure adaptation requires.

  • Capacity grows faster than cost. For many systems, such as pipes, tanks, and treatment plants, capacity grows faster than the cost of building them. Similarly, a seawall or cooling network protects far more people per dollar in a dense city than across a dispersed population.
  • Cheaper essential services. Clean water, sanitation, and reliable electricity are critical during and after a climate shock, and they cost less to deliver per person in dense urban areas.

Specialized skills and services

Large cities support a fine-grained division of labor, which means they can sustain firms that focus on environmental risk assessment, climate-resilient architecture, and building retrofits. Smaller, less dense places rarely have access to that kind of specialized expertise, so cities can respond to climate threats with more sophistication.

How agglomeration makes adaptation harder

The same concentration that builds adaptive capacity also concentrates risk.

Concentrated risk and exposure

Agglomeration puts people, economic activity, and critical infrastructure in one place. That creates an “all eggs in one basket” problem, where a single event can be devastating.

  • Bigger disasters. A hurricane, flood, or heat wave that hits a dense city affects far more people and causes far more economic damage than the same event in a sparsely populated area, as Hurricane Katrina showed in New Orleans in 2005. Nearly two-thirds of urban settlements with more than 5 million residents lie at least partly in low-elevation coastal zones, which leaves them especially exposed to sea-level rise and storm surges (McGranahan et al., 2007).
  • Cascading failures. The interconnectedness that makes urban life efficient also makes it fragile. Damage to a single power station, water treatment plant, or transit hub can trigger failures across an entire metropolitan system.

“Sterile” agglomeration

In many cities in the developing world, the potential benefits of agglomeration are overwhelmed by congestion, pollution, inadequate housing, and crime. This is sometimes called sterile agglomeration: high density without the matching gains in productivity. These cities are crowded rather than productive.

Climate risk deepens urban inequality. The urban poor are often concentrated in informal settlements, such as those in Mumbai, located in the most hazardous areas, including floodplains and unstable hillsides, with the least access to protective infrastructure and services. Climate shocks fall hardest on the most vulnerable, which undermines adaptation for the city as a whole.

Locked in by location

Many cities were founded centuries ago because of geographic advantages, such as coastal ports, that have now become climate liabilities. The pull of agglomeration keeps them where they are. Relocating a major city like New Orleans or Mexico City would be prohibitively expensive, even though both face serious climate risks. This path dependence forces cities into costly “protect-in-place” strategies that may eventually become unsustainable.

The bottom line

Agglomeration creates a double-edged challenge for climate adaptation. The concentration of knowledge, capital, and expertise in thriving cities can drive innovation and resilience, but it also concentrates risk and locks cities into vulnerable locations. The challenge is sharpest in the developing world, where weak urban conditions limit adaptive capacity.

Ultimately, a city’s ability to capture the benefits of agglomeration while managing its downsides depends on the quality of its urbanization. Productive, inclusive cities are better positioned to adapt. Cities where agglomeration fails to deliver will find climate change makes their existing problems worse.
